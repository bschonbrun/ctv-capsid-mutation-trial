# Proposed Mutation of the Citrus Tristeza Virus Minor Coat Protein to Disable Aphid Transmission While Preserving Virion Assembly and Plant Movement

**Prepared for:** CTV vector and disease management research group  
**Subject:** First-trial mutation design in the CTV CPm gene  
**Objective:** Identify a capsid mutation that separates aphid transmissibility from virion assembly and systemic plant infection

---

## 1. Summary

The recommended first-trial mutation for Citrus tristeza virus (CTV) is a **deletion of the N-terminal 2–67 amino acids of the minor coat protein CPm** in the infectious clone T36:

> **CTV-T36 CPm Δ2–67**

This mutation is expected to preserve virion self-assembly and systemic movement within the plant, but to abolish aphid-mediated plant-to-plant transmission.

The rationale is that CTV virions are polar: most of the particle is coated by the major coat protein p25, while one end—the “tail”—contains the minor coat protein CPm together with p65 and p61. Aphid transmission of CTV depends on specific interactions between the exposed N-terminal portion of CPm and the aphid foregut. Removing this domain should uncouple transmission from assembly and movement.

---

## 2. Background

Citrus tristeza virus is a member of the genus *Closterovirus*, family *Closteroviridae*. Its flexuous filamentous virions have a complex architecture. The major coat protein p25 covers the majority of the particle, while the minor coat protein CPm, along with p65 and p61, forms a distinct tail structure at one end.

Aphid transmission of CTV is non-circulatory and semi-persistent. Virions are acquired when the aphid feeds on phloem sap, bind to the cuticle of the aphid foregut, and are later released during subsequent feeding. This binding is mediated in part by the tail region, and particularly by the surface-exposed N-terminal domain of CPm.

Reverse-genetics studies using full-length CTV infectious clones have shown that CPm is essential for virion assembly and systemic infection, but that different regions of CPm have separable functions. The N-terminal portion is a transmission determinant, while the C-terminal portion is important for interactions with p65, p61, and the viral RNA during assembly.

---

## 3. Rationale for Targeting CPm

CTV CPm is a more attractive first target than the major coat protein p25 because:

- p25 coats the entire virion body; many mutations in p25 can destabilize particle assembly or reduce plant movement.
- CPm is concentrated at one virion end and is a known aphid transmission determinant.
- The N-terminal domain of CPm is exposed and likely not required for core tail assembly.
- Deletion of the CPm N-terminal domain is predicted to remove aphid-binding residues without disrupting the C-terminal interactions needed for tail formation.

This creates an opportunity to uncouple aphid transmission from assembly and movement.

---

## 4. Proposed Mutation

**Mutation:**  
Delete amino acids 2 through 67 from the N-terminus of CTV CPm in the T36 infectious clone.

The deletion removes the surface-exposed N-terminal domain while retaining the start codon and the remaining C-terminal portion of CPm.

If the full Δ2–67 deletion causes unexpected defects in assembly or plant movement, a more conservative fallback is to perform **alanine-scanning mutagenesis** within CPm residues 1–50, focusing on clustered charged or polar residues such as aspartate, glutamate, lysine, and arginine. Mutants should then be screened for:

- normal virion assembly,
- normal systemic movement in citrus,
- loss of aphid transmission by *Toxoptera citricida* or *Aphis gossypii*.

---

## 5. Expected Phenotype

| Property | Expected outcome |
|---|---|
| Virion self-assembly | Maintained |
| Cell-to-cell movement in plant | Maintained |
| Systemic movement in citrus | Maintained |
| Aphid acquisition / transmission | Lost or strongly reduced |
| Plant-to-plant spread by aphids | Disabled |

The remaining C-terminal region of CPm is expected to remain competent for interaction with p65, p61, and the major coat protein, allowing tail formation and virion assembly.

---

## 6. Validation Plan

After generating the mutant clone and obtaining infected citrus tissue, the following assays are recommended:

1. **Virion assembly**  
   - Examine purified virions by transmission electron microscopy.  
   - Confirm presence of p25 and CPm by Western blot.

2. **Systemic plant infection**  
   - Use RT-PCR or immunodetection to confirm systemic movement in citrus seedlings.  
   - Compare symptom development and virus titer with wild-type T36.

3. **Aphid transmission assay**  
   - Use *Toxoptera citricida* and *Aphis gossypii* in acquisition access and inoculation access experiments.  
   - Include wild-type T36 as a positive control.  
   - Confirm loss of transmission while the mutant remains mechanically or graft transmissible.

4. **Fitness stability**  
   - Pass the mutant through several systemic infections to check for reversion or compensatory mutations in CPm, p65, or p61.

---

## 7. Risks and Alternative Approaches

**Risk 1:** The Δ2–67 deletion may affect tail assembly more than expected, reducing virion accumulation or plant movement.

- Mitigation: Test smaller deletions, e.g. Δ2–20, Δ2–40, or internal alanine substitutions within the N-terminal 50 residues.

**Risk 2:** Aphid transmission may be reduced but not fully abolished, because other tail proteins or the major coat protein may also contribute to foregut binding.

- Mitigation: Combine CPm N-terminal mutations with targeted mutations in p65 or p61 if a complete transmission block is required.

**Risk 3:** The mutant may be less stable in citrus or may not be competitive in field conditions.

- Mitigation: Evaluate stability over multiple passages and in mixed infections; use the mutant as a biological control or cross-protection agent only if stable.

---

## 8. Conclusions and Recommendations

The CPm N-terminal deletion **CTV-T36 CPm Δ2–67** is the best first-trial mutation for uncoupling aphid transmission from virion assembly and systemic plant movement. It is based on the known polar architecture of the CTV virion and the role of the CPm tail in aphid transmission.

**Recommendations:**

1. Generate the Δ2–67 mutant in the T36 infectious clone and inoculate citrus.
2. Confirm virion assembly by electron microscopy and Western blot.
3. Confirm systemic infection by RT-PCR.
4. Perform aphid transmission assays with *T. citricida* and *A. gossypii*.
5. If transmission is not fully blocked, test progressive smaller deletions or alanine-scanning mutations within the CPm N-terminus.
6. Evaluate mutant stability and fitness before considering field deployment.

This strategy provides a rational path toward a CTV variant that is retained in infected plants but cannot spread naturally by aphids, which may be useful for cross-protection, vector-transmission research, and disease management.
